1. The model and its geometry

We work with the \(D=4\) Villain model on a periodic hypercubic lattice: a real 0-form \(\varphi\) on sites and an integer 1-form \(n\) on links, with action \[ S \;=\; \frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_\ell \bigl(d\varphi - 2\pi n\bigr)_\ell^2 . \] The vorticity is the integer 2-form \(F = dn\) on plaquettes; its dual is the vortex worldsheet, a closed 2-surface on the dual lattice. The No-Intersection model imposes on every hypercube the constraint \[ q \;=\; dn \wedge dn \;=\; 0, \] which forbids (signed) self-intersections of the worldsheet. Two lattice facts shape everything that follows.

The lattice wedge carries a shift. With plaquette components indexed by ordered pairs, \[ (a\wedge b)_{(0123)}[x] \;=\; \sum_{A\sqcup B}\sigma(A\!\frown\! B)\; a_A[x]\; b_B[x+\hat e_A], \qquad \hat e_A = \textstyle\sum_{k\in A}\hat e_k , \] the second factor is evaluated at a shifted corner. Consequences: the intersection density is a pairing of complementary plaquettes \(\{(01),(23)\},\{(02),(13)\},\{(03),(12)\}\) at diagonally offset positions; and single-axis reflections are not exact symmetries of \(q\) (the shift breaks them, the same phenomenon by which \(\star\star\) acquires a lattice shift). Axis permutations, full parity, and the global negation \(n\to-n\) (since \(q\) is quadratic) are exact. This little fact will bite in §4.

\(q\) is quadratic in \(n\), so charge changes linearize. For any sparse change \(\Delta n\) with \(\Delta F = d(\Delta n)\), \[ \Delta q \;=\; \Delta F\wedge F \;+\; F\wedge \Delta F \;+\; \Delta F\wedge \Delta F , \] and every term is supported within a couple of cells of the changed links. All constraint checks in the code go through this local \(\Delta q\) (no_intersection/charge.py: local_dq), covered by randomized local-vs-global tests on cluttered worm backgrounds and along an accepted single-link trajectory. A single proposal costs \(O(1)\) in the volume; the field strength \(F\) is maintained incrementally.

A special case used constantly: if \(\Delta n\) changes links of only one direction \(\mu\), then every component of \(\Delta F\) contains \(\mu\), so no two are complementary and \[ \Delta F\wedge\Delta F \;\equiv\; 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta q \ \text{is exactly linear in } \Delta n . \] On a vacuum background (\(F=0\)) such a change can never create charge; on a nonzero background the violations come only from the cross terms.

2. Why ordinary local Metropolis struggles

A single-link update \(n_\ell \to n_\ell \pm 1\) is harmless where \(F=0\) nearby, but on a background carrying flux the cross terms light up charge, and the proposal must be rejected. Three escalating symptoms:

  1. Dilute sheets: near a moderate-density worldsheet roughly a third of local proposals are charge-clean — annoying but workable.
  2. Dense phases: at \(\sim\)40% plaquette occupancy the clean fraction collapses to \(\sim 10^{-3}\); local dynamics effectively stops.
  3. Frozen configurations (§5): there exist valid configurations with zero legal bounded-support moves of any kind. Escaping them requires coordinated moves whose shape depends on the background — which is exactly what worms provide.

The alternative of proposing large coordinated moves atomically fails for the usual reason: a torus-wrapping loop changes \(O(N)\) links at once, so its acceptance dies like \(e^{-c\kappa N}\). Measured at \(N=6\): the atomic WrappingLoopUpdate was accepted 0 times in 4000 proposals on thermalized backgrounds at \(\kappa = 0.1\) and \(0.2\); the rare charge-clean loops carried median \(\Delta S \approx +13\) and \(+24\) respectively.

3. The worm skeleton both algorithms share

Both worms are Prokof'ev–Svistunov algorithms on an extended ensemble \(Z \cup G\): the physical sector \(Z\) of valid configurations, and an auxiliary sector \(G\) in which a specified defect pair (a head and a tail) is permitted. One compound update ("episode") is:

  1. Insert head and tail on the same uniformly random cell (\(\Delta S = 0\)).
  2. Move the head, one elementary step at a time. Each step is drawn from a state-independent menu such that every move’s exact inverse appears on the reverse menu with the same probability; each is filtered by a locality/cleanliness condition (a factor in the \(G\)-sector weight) and then Metropolis-tested with \(\min(1, e^{-\Delta S})\).
  3. Close: whenever the state is closable (head on tail, constraint restored), closure is offered as the \((2D{+}1)\)-th equally likely option; if taken, the configuration is emitted into the Markov chain.
The invariant to keep in mind. The stored Markov chain never contains a constraint-violating configuration. Violations live only inside a worm episode, in the auxiliary \(G\)-sector. A proposal that would move to a disallowed \(G\)-state is a null proposal, and a completed update is emitted only from a closable state in \(Z\), where \(q\equiv0\). The two worms differ only in how tightly the temporary defect pattern is controlled while the head is open.
Why detailed balance holds. Consider a completed episode visiting states \(s_0,\dots,s_k\) (both endpoints closable). Its probability is a product of per-step factors; the reversed path is built from the same menu. Two things need checking. (i) Per step, proposal probabilities pair up exactly (each move vs. its inverse), cleanliness of a move implies cleanliness of its inverse, and the Metropolis ratio supplies \(e^{-\Delta S}\). (ii) The diagonal bookkeeping: closable states use step probability \(\tfrac1{2D+1}\) instead of \(\tfrac1{2D}\). The forward product involves the closability of \(s_0,\dots,s_{k-1}\) and the reverse that of \(s_1,\dots,s_k\); since \(s_0\) and \(s_k\) are both closable, the two factor sets coincide and cancel. Insertion (\(\propto 1/V\)) and closure (\(\tfrac1{2D+1}\)) appear once on each side. Net: \(P(\text{path})/P(\text{reversed}) = e^{-\Delta S_{\text{total}}}\), so the emitted \(Z\)-sector configurations sample \(e^{-S}\) restricted to the constraint surface.

Everything else — and everything that went wrong in the naive versions — is about which defect the head carries, which elementary steps are on the menu, and what weight the \(G\)-sector states get.

4. The IntersectionWorm

4.1 What it does

The defect is a unit of intersection charge: head and tail live on hypercubes and carry \(q = +1\) and \(-1\). Moving the head one hypercube requires a coordinated few-link change of \(n\) whose \(\Delta q\) is a clean dipole, \(\{ \text{target}:+1,\ \text{head}:-1 \}\), verified on the live background by the local \(\Delta q\). A proposal draws one of the \(2D\) directions uniformly and one shape uniformly from a fixed library bucket for that direction; the menu for a step in direction \(d\) consists of the bucket-\(d\) shapes anchored at the target plus the negated bucket-\((-d)\) shapes anchored at the head — the latter are precisely the exact inverses of forward steps that could have arrived at the current state, which is what makes the proposal-pairing in §3 work. Unclean draws are simply rejected (the head stalls), which is detailed-balance safe. When the head returns to the tail the two defects cancel and the configuration is exactly valid.

Equivalently, the IntersectionWorm carries a one-line defect ledger. After insertion the ledger is empty because head and tail coincide. The first accepted step creates \(\{\text{head}:+1,\text{ tail}:-1\}\). Every later accepted step contributes \(\{\text{new head}:+1,\text{old head}:-1\}\): the \(-1\) cancels the old \(+1\), leaving only the same dipole with the \(+1\) moved to the new head. The code does not explicitly store this ledger; it enforces it by requiring _dq(F, change) == {target: 1, head: -1} for each accepted shape. On the closing step, the new head is the tail, so the \(+1\) cancels the fixed tail \(-1\), and the emitted configuration has no intersection charge.

Anatomy of a move. Only the head moves: the tail is a fixed spectator, holding its \(-1\) until the head comes back to annihilate it. (This mirrors the classic 2d worm; one could equally well evolve the tail, but nothing is gained.) An accepted step changes two or three links simultaneously — of the 828 shapes per direction, 816 are 3-link and 12 are 2-link — and the Metropolis test uses the Villain \(\Delta S\) of just those links. For instance the 2-link shape \((n_0[t]\,{-}1,\; n_1[t+\hat e_0+\hat e_2]\,{+}1)\), anchored at the target hypercube \(t\), moves the \(+1\) defect one unit in \(+\hat e_3\); the generic 3-link shapes add one more link, e.g. \(n_0[t-\hat e_3]\,{-}1\). A drawn shape counts as a move only if its local \(\Delta q\) is exactly \(\{t:+1,\ \text{head}:-1\}\); the \(+1\) rides with the head by convention, and any other outcome (extra defects, wrong signs) is a stall.

Physically, an open intersection worm is an immersed worldsheet with a \(\pm1\) intersection pair — transporting the head and annihilating it against the tail implements finger/Whitney-type moves. This is why the worm connects 2-knot sectors that bounded constraint-preserving moves cannot: it is the algorithmic finger move.

Finger and Whitney moves. In smooth four-dimensional topology, a finger move pushes a small finger of one two-dimensional sheet through another. During the push the surface is no longer embedded: it has become an immersed surface with two new transverse intersection points of opposite sign. That is the role of the IntersectionWorm’s head and tail. The worm temporarily carries a \(+1/-1\) intersection pair, then uses clean local dipole steps to transport the \(+1\) head through the configuration.

A Whitney move is the complementary cancellation. If two opposite-sign intersections are connected by suitable arcs on the sheets, those arcs bound a Whitney disk; pushing across that disk removes the pair and returns to an embedded surface. The lattice worm does not construct a smooth disk, but the closure rule is the discrete analog: when the head reaches the tail, the \(+1\) and \(-1\) entries in the local \(q\)-ledger annihilate, and only the resulting \(q\equiv0\) worldsheet is emitted. The useful point is the same in both languages: to move between embedded surfaces, it can be essential to pass through a controlled immersed intermediate state.

4.2 Subtlety: the move library was starved — and lattice symmetry is smaller than you think

Symptom. The original library held the orbit of one hand-found 3-link shape under the 24 axis permutations, and even discarded half of that orbit: 3 shapes per direction. The head was far from "moving freely."

Three separate issues were tangled together:

  • Re-anchoring. Odd permutations flip which end of the dipole carries the \(+1\). The naive code required the \(+1\) to land on the template’s anchor and threw those away; the fix is to re-anchor every transformed template on its \(+1\) defect.
  • Negation. Since \(q\) is quadratic, \(-\Delta n\) produces the same dipole on an empty background but is a genuinely different move (different links change). The naive library missed all of them.
  • Reflections are not symmetries. A first attempt asserted that all 384 signed axis permutations preserve dipole cleanliness — and the assertion fired. Single-axis reflections do not commute with the wedge’s \(x+\hat e_A\) shift (§1). Transformed candidates must be filtered by re-validating on an empty background, never assumed clean.
Resolution: enumerate, don’t guess. example/no-intersection-move-search.py exhaustively enumerates every change of at most 3 links with coefficients \(\pm1\) whose \(\Delta q\) on an empty background is a clean unit dipole. Result: 828 distinct moves per direction, falling into 41 symmetry classes (§4.3) — the original seed’s full equivalence class covers just 12 of the 828 (1.4%). The class representatives are stored in the auto-generated moves.py; the library builder expands them under the signed permutations and negation with the empty-background filter, and reproduces exactly 828 per bucket in all 8 directions — a nontrivial consistency check between the orbit machinery and the brute-force search, pinned by a test.
A physics surprise. The minimal clean move has two links, not three. The old documentation reasoned that moving the head across a 3-cube requires a coordinated 3-link change; the search found two 2-link classes (their dipole arises purely from the \(\Delta F\wedge\Delta F\) cross term). Being one link cheaper, their bare acceptance is a factor \(e^{2\pi^2\kappa/\dots}\sim 7\times\) better at \(\kappa=0.1\).
What library size does and does not buy. Measured near a moderate-density worldsheet, the clean-proposal ("stall") rate barely moved: \(\approx 0.36\) with 3 shapes/direction vs. \(\approx 0.34\) with 828. Stalling is controlled by the local geometry, not the catalog size. What completeness buys is coverage — 69× more distinct sheet-dragging maneuvers per step, including the cheap 2-link ones — i.e. ergodicity headroom, not a lower rejection rate. (§4.3 quantifies exactly this with coverage measurements.)

4.3 The elementary moves: minimality, completeness, and the 41 classes

Why two and three links? Work up from the smallest support. A single link can never be an elementary move: its \(\Delta F\) lives on the six plaquettes containing that link, every one of which contains the link’s direction \(\mu\) — so no two of them are complementary, \(\Delta F\wedge\Delta F\equiv 0\), and one link creates no charge on an empty background (Fig 7; the exhaustive search confirms: zero 1-link moves). Charge creation therefore needs \(\Delta F\) in two complementary planes, hence at least two links of distinct directions, and placed just so: exactly 12 shapes per direction achieve the clean dipole this way, and they are the action-cheapest moves in the library. The generic elementary move has three links (816 shapes per direction, spread over ten direction-patterns). The cap at three is a design judgment, not a theorem: each additional fresh link multiplies the bare acceptance by \(e^{-2\pi^2\kappa}\) (a factor \(\sim 7\) at \(\kappa=0.1\)), and long coordinated maneuvers are better built by chaining elementary moves through the \(G\)-sector — which is what a worm is for — than by enumerating ever-larger atomic proposals.

Completeness. The library is complete by direct enumeration within a precisely stated class: every \(\Delta n\) supported on at most 3 links, with coefficients \(\pm1\), whose \(\Delta q\) on the empty background is a clean unit dipole (example/no-intersection-move-search.py). The enumeration takes all 1-, 2- and 3-subsets of the links that can influence the charge of the two defect hypercubes. The certified class is that on-target census plus its transform-orbit closure: as discovered during the unification of §8, the filtered transforms can carry an all-on-target move to a clean move with one off-target link whose cross terms cancel in context, so the closure — which is exactly what the library builder constructs — is the honest boundary of the class. (For the face displacement the closure adds nothing: 828 stays 828.) One dipole direction is enumerated; the other seven buckets follow from axis permutations and full parity, which are exact symmetries of \(q\). As an independent cross-check, the library builder regenerates exactly 828 shapes per direction in all 8 buckets from the stored representatives — two very different computations agreeing on the count, pinned by a CI test. Three honest caveats: coefficients \(\pm2\) and beyond are not enumerated (they cost \(4\times\) the action per link); supports of 4+ links are not enumerated; and “clean” is judged on the empty background. Moves that are clean only against a particular background exist — even a single link can be, when the cross terms conspire — but they cannot live in a fixed, state-independent catalog without Metropolis–Hastings corrections; the worm reaches such background-adapted maneuvers by composition instead.

Where the classes come from. Call two moves equivalent when a symmetry of the constraint maps one to the other: a lattice translation, a signed axis permutation (the hyperoctahedral group of the hypercube, \(4!\times 2^4 = 384\) elements), or the global negation \(n\to-n\) (legal because \(q\) is quadratic). Translations are absorbed by re-anchoring every template on its \(+1\) defect, leaving \(384\times 2 = 768\) transforms. Two caveats make this a filtered action rather than a clean group action on the library: only the 96-element subgroup generated by permutations, parity, and negation is an exact symmetry of the lattice \(q\) (single-axis reflections fail, by the wedge shift of §1), so a transform can carry a clean move to an unclean template that the filter discards; and an orbit spreads over all eight direction buckets, so we classify within one bucket. The result, verified numerically as an exact partition: the 828 moves per direction fall into 41 equivalence classes,

with sizes \(6\,(\times3)\), \(12\,(\times18)\), \(18\,(\times1)\), \(24\,(\times14)\), \(48\,(\times5)\).

Implementation status. The current generated file stores the reduced form directly: moves.py contains 41 seed representatives. Of these, 2 are two-link representatives and 39 are three-link representatives. Expanding those seeds and filtering on the empty-background dipole condition reproduces exactly 828 shapes in each of the 8 unit-direction buckets, which is the count asserted by test_intersection_worm_library_covers_all_directions.

Would a smaller set do? Three answers for three senses of “smaller.” Under symmetry, the library is 41 classes — but that is bookkeeping, not redundancy: at proposal time, orbit-mates anchored at the same head are genuinely different moves (different links, different \(\Delta S\), independently blocked or clean on a live background), and likewise each shape and its negation. 4.4 Subtlety: the global charge recomputation

Symptom. The naive implementation recomputed the global charge \(q = dn\wedge dn\) (\(O(\text{volume})\)) once per proposal — and twice per proposal in the single-link update, making a sweep \(O(V^2)\).
Resolution. The linearized local \(\Delta q\) of §1, plus incremental maintenance of \(F\). Every generator now checks constraints at \(O(1)\) (worm, link update) or \(O(N)\) (wrapping loops) cost. The single-link sweep went from volume-scaling to a flat \(\sim\!18\,\mu s\) per link.

4.5 Validation

  • Local vs. global \(\Delta q\): exact agreement on 20 randomized cluttered worm backgrounds (including \(|n| \ge 2\) and invalid ones); the single-link local constraint check also matches global validity through a 300-trial accepted trajectory.
  • Move inversion: every forward library placement is exactly undone by the negated placement (the pairing detailed balance rests on) — tested.
  • Distribution: ensembles generated by Hammers with and without the worm agree on action density, winding\(^2\), wrapping\(^2\) (pulls \(\le 0.8\sigma\)); every emitted configuration valid.
  • Mechanism: seeded with an explicitly-constructed spun-trefoil worldsheet (a knotted embedded 2-sphere with \(q\equiv0\), see example/spun-trefoil.py), the worm freely dissolves and reorganizes the knotted sheet in the condensed phase with validity maintained throughout.

5. Interlude: frozen configurations

Evan’s constructions (example/no-intersection-frozen.py) build valid configurations with space-filling staggered flux — e.g. the single-pair family \(F_{01}\propto(-1)^{x_0}\), \(F_{23}\propto(-1)^{x_0+x_2+x_3}\), where \(q\) cancels between the two wedge orderings; and the six-plane family \(F_{\mu\nu} = A_{\mu\nu}(-1)^{x_\mu+x_\nu}\) with \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)=0\). Why locality must fail: the extremal-plaquette argument. The backgrounds are space-filling. Any \(\Delta n\) of bounded support has an extremal \(\Delta F\) plaquette; if it lies in a plane whose complement is lit, its cross term against the everywhere-nonzero background deposits charge at the extremal corner, where nothing else can cancel it. A legal move must therefore have \(\Delta F\equiv0\) in every plane complementary to a lit one — e.g. \(\Delta F_{01}=\Delta F_{23}=0\) for the single-pair family, i.e. \(\Delta n_0\) constant along \(x_1\) — and constancy along a torus direction forces the support to wrap. The minimal legal moves are the length-\(N\) rings, which is exactly the axis-ring branch of WrappingLoopUpdate. (Note: since only one link direction changes, \(\Delta q\) is exactly linear, and the legal rings are kernel vectors of that linear map.)

Two sobering measurements sharpen the picture at \(\kappa\ne0\). First, the atomic ring update is essentially never accepted on generic backgrounds (0/4000, §2). Second, even on the frozen texture itself, no clean ring is downhill: of 231 clean proposals in 4000, all had \(\Delta S > 0\). A ring’s \(\Delta F\) is a dipole — opposite flux tubes at adjacent transverse positions — so on a saturated staggered background it always cancels one tube’s worth and augments the neighbor’s. The frozen texture at \(\kappa>0\) is a genuine local action minimum. Because the interior blocking argument is local, a finite patch of such texture is equally locked from within at any \(\kappa\); its action is extensive in the patch volume, so this is a metastability issue (quenches, seeded starts, corrupted autocorrelations), not an equilibrium-bias one. This is the problem the StringWorm was built for.

6. The StringWorm

6.1 What it does

The StringWorm is the incremental version of the coordinated single-direction moves. An episode fixes \((\mu, s)\) and inserts head = tail on a random site. Each step moves the head one unit in a uniformly random direction \(\pm\hat e_\nu\) (any \(\nu\), including \(\mu\)) and deposits \(\pm s\) on the \(\mu\)-link it crosses — the classic 2d-worm crossing rule, so stepping back undoes the deposit and every step’s inverse is on the reverse menu with probability \(\tfrac18\). Since every step changes only \(n_\mu\), \(\Delta F\wedge\Delta F\equiv0\) (§1): on vacuum the string creates no charge at all, and on a background any charge comes from the cross terms with the existing \(F\). Those charges are tracked in an explicit defect ledger, a sparse dictionary of hypercube charges updated by local_dq after each proposed crossed-link deposit. In contrast to the IntersectionWorm’s coordinated 2–3-link shapes, each StringWorm step changes exactly one link; and as there, only the head moves — the tail marks the insertion site until the episode closes.

The important point is that the soft constraint is not the model being sampled. It is only the weight of open-string states inside the auxiliary sector. A proposed step computes \(\Delta q_{\text{local}}\), merges it into the current ledger, deletes any entries whose charge has canceled to zero, and then asks two questions before the Metropolis test: are all remaining violations within radius \(r\) of the head or tail, and is \(\sum_x |q_x|\) below the ceiling? If not, the proposal is a null move. If yes, the Metropolis action includes both the Villain link cost and the fugacity change \(\lambda(\sum|\text{new defects}|-\sum|\text{old defects}|)\). Later steps clean the violation by adding opposite local \(\Delta q\) contributions; exact backtracking is the simplest example, because it removes the last deposited link and exactly reverses its defect contribution. The worm can emit only when the head is back on the tail and the ledger is empty.

Its emitted \(Z\)-sector outcomes are of two kinds: wrapped strings (the rings of §5, now built stepwise) and — the real payoff — contractible closed strings: local, coordinated multi-link moves of arbitrary background-adapted shape, which no fixed catalog can enumerate. These are exactly what a frozen droplet’s boundary needs.

The naive version of this algorithm failed in three distinct, instructive ways.

6.2 Subtlety: the charged-blob catastrophe

Symptom. With the \(G\)-sector weighted by \(e^{-S}\) times a (roomy, radius-3) confinement indicator, episodes locked into states carrying 8–17 persistent violations and \(\sim90\) units of deposited links, with the head hovering near the tail for millions of iterations.

Diagnosis. Charge violations cost nothing in that ensemble — only the action of the deposited links enters \(\Delta S\). The set of confined charged states is combinatorially enormous (a Chebyshev-radius-3 ball holds \(7^4 = 2401\) sites), so entropy favors a messy endpoint blob over the measure-zero-ish constraint surface. The IntersectionWorm never had this failure mode because its per-move cleanliness filter admits exactly one dipole; the StringWorm’s permissive endpoint structure re-opened it.

Resolution: charge the defects rent. The \(G\)-sector weight is now \[ w_G \;\propto\; e^{-S \;-\; \lambda \sum_x |q_x|}\times \chi\bigl(\text{violations within radius } r \text{ of head or tail};\ \textstyle\sum|q| \le \text{ceiling}\bigr). \] The fugacity \(\lambda\) (default 0.5) makes each unit of violation cost \(e^{-\lambda}\) in the step acceptance, and the ceiling bounds the state space outright. Crucially, all of this reweights or restricts only the auxiliary sector: every emission has \(\sum|q| = 0\), so the physical \(Z\)-marginal is untouched. The fugacity also does not "repair" a violation by fiat: it only changes the odds of accepting a step. Repair means literal cancellation in the sparse defect ledger, after later local \(\Delta q\) updates have brought every entry back to zero. The radius default \(r=2\) is measured, not guessed: a single deposit’s violations on the staggered background reach Chebyshev distance 1 of the link (2 of the head) with \(\sum|q|\) up to 8.

6.3 Subtlety: the algorithm has its own phase transition

Symptom. Even after the blob fix, episode lengths at \(\kappa=0.1\) had wild outliers (\(3\times10^4\)–\(10^6\) iterations), getting worse as \(\kappa\) decreased.

Diagnosis. From a fresh region, depositing a link costs \(\Delta S = \tfrac{\kappa}{2}(2\pi)^2 = 2\pi^2\kappa\), while retracting the last deposit is free (and always accepted when proposed). Per iteration the head has \(2D-1 = 7\) "outward" options accepted with probability \(e^{-2\pi^2\kappa}\) against essentially one inward one: the excursion is a branching random walk that goes critical when \[ (2D-1)\, e^{-2\pi^2\kappa} \;=\; 1 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \kappa^\ast \;=\; \frac{\ln 7}{2\pi^2} \;\approx\; 0.099 . \] Below and near \(\kappa^\ast\) the return times acquire the heavy tails of critical branching (the free 4d walk is transient), and mean episode lengths diverge. It is amusing — and worth remembering — that the sampler has a sharp transition at a coupling with a closed-form expression.

Resolution: respect the domain. No knob removes this; it is the physics of the extended ensemble. The StringWorm is a \(\kappa \gtrsim 0.12\) tool (safely subcritical), which is precisely the stiff regime where the frozen-region worry lives. The \(\kappa\approx0\) regime — where Evan’s search operates and where atomic proposals are accepted freely — stays with WrappingLoopUpdate. The docstring carries the formula.

6.4 Subtlety: entropic stewing in flat landscapes

Symptom. Even safely subcritical (\(\kappa = 0.2\)), rare episodes ran for \(10^5\)–\(3\times10^6\) iterations: after earlier episodes deposit debris, the head finds flat directions (moves canceling existing flux cost \(\approx 0\)) and diffuses in a labyrinth of equal-action states. Neither the fugacity (the states are uncharged) nor directed proposal schemes (the options are genuinely equal-weight) can help; the problem is entropic.
Resolution: abort-and-discard, which is exactly detailed-balance safe. Episodes exceeding a cap (default 5000 iterations) are aborted and the entire episode is discarded — the pre-episode configuration is returned. Why this introduces no bias: the bijection between a completed path and its reverse preserves the total iteration count, because stall probabilities depend only on the state being stalled at, and the reversed path visits the same multiset of states. Truncating at the cap therefore removes forward and reverse paths in pairs, and what remains still satisfies \(P(\text{path})/P(\text{reversed}) = e^{-\Delta S}\); aborted weight lands harmlessly on the diagonal of the transition kernel. (Contrast: aborting and keeping the partial string would emit invalid configurations; aborting and keeping a valid intermediate state would break the pairing. Discarding is the unique cheap option that is exactly safe.)

With all three fixes the worm is healthy: median episode 5–6 iterations, means of 20–80, a bounded 5–18% of episodes hitting the cap, and every emission valid, at all tested couplings \(\kappa \in [0.12, 0.2]\). An optional reach knob (max head–tail Chebyshev distance, a pure state restriction, hence DB-safe) can further localize the update if desired.

6.5 Validation — including a cautionary tale about baselines

Symptom. The ensemble cross-check (chains with vs. without the StringWorm, \(N=4\), \(\kappa=0.15\)) initially showed a correlated \(-2.1\sigma\) pull in the action density. Alarming for a sampler.
Resolution: the baseline was the biased one. Replication with independent seeds showed the without-worm chains scattered 3× wider than their binned errors claimed — slow mixing — and sat low, the signature of cold-start under-thermalization. Run to 4000 sweeps, the baseline drifts up and plateaus at 1.0989(3), statistically consistent (\(\sim1\sigma\)) with the worm-assisted chains’ 1.1021(20), which had plateaued almost immediately. The "discrepancy" was the worm-assisted chain thermalizing faster. Moral: when a new update seems to shift an observable, first ask whether the reference chain has actually converged. One long production-scale validation run is still recommended before leaning on the StringWorm at scale.

The worms make that machinery less likely to be needed; they do not make it redundant.

8. Postscript: convergent evolution and the unified worm

While this document was being written, Evan was independently developing his own IntersectionWorm on origin/feature/no-intersection-model — and the two lines attacked the same weaknesses from opposite directions. His branch grew the move set by hand-discovery: a 2-link orthogonal move (the two links, surprisingly, share no corner), a 2-link opposite-sign-diagonal “elbow,” and a 4-link same-sign-diagonal move — teaching the head to walk a richer neighbour graph — while keeping the global \(O(\text{volume})\) charge check. Ours grew the move set by exhaustive enumeration within the unit-displacement class and made every constraint check local, but had filtered out diagonal displacements entirely. Verified relationships: his orthogonal families are strict subsets of our 828 (every clean orbit member present); his diagonal families were entirely outside our move class. Each side had found what the other's blind spot hid.

His parameter scans also supplied the empirical sweet-spot anatomy of §2 and §4 from the physics side: at high \(\kappa\) the worm is Metropolis-starved (the cheapest move costs two links, \(e^{-4\pi^2\kappa}\)), at low \(\kappa\) it is constraint-starved (vortex proliferation makes almost every shape unclean), and the product peaks near \(\kappa \approx 0.1\). One diagnostic caution his data make vivid: the Intersection_Intersection correlator is measured inline by the worm itself, so where the worm jams, the correlator’s long-distance bins are not small — they are unmeasured. Plot the worm-length distribution next to the correlator, and trust \(I\)-\(I(\Delta x)\) only where the head’s excursions actually reach \(\Delta x\). On the physics: \(e^{i\theta}\) is the disorder operator for intersection charge, so Spin–Spin long-range order at large \(\kappa\) should be mirrored by intersection-charge condensation at small \(\kappa\) — dual order parameters.

The branch feature/no-intersection-unified merges the two designs. The head walks the 32-neighbour graph (8 face steps, 24 signed 2-plane diagonals); every bucket is filled by the census machinery; every check is the local \(\Delta q\):

The unification found one more completeness subtlety, caught by an assertion: the elbow census’s orbit closure is larger than its on-target enumeration (540 vs 412), because a filtered transform can map an all-on-target move to a clean move carrying one link that touches neither defect hypercube — its cross terms cancel in context. A clean 2-link elbow plus such a context-neutral third link is a genuine 3-link move that no on-target enumeration can see. The certified class is therefore the on-target census plus its orbit closure, which is precisely the set the library builder constructs; for the face displacement the closure happens to add nothing. Also merged from Evan’s branch: the global PlanarFluxUpdate (decomposable \(A = u\wedge v\) sheet tunneling, now in the Hammer) and the relocated frozen-configuration examples with the \(u\wedge v\) parametrization of the six-plane family.
